# Copilot for Business — a Two-Hour Overview
*v1.0.0*

**Goal for this session:** by the end of two hours you can use Copilot for real work. You'll write prompts that get usable output on the first or second try, draft and summarize email, documents and meetings, analyze a small table, and catch Copilot when it's wrong. You'll also know what never goes into it. You'll practise on your own work, not made-up examples, so you leave with things you can actually use.

**Time budget:** ~110 min of hands-on exercises, ~10 min journal. One sitting, with a short break around the one-hour mark.

**What you need:** the **Microsoft 365 Copilot app** signed in with your **work account**. Have three real things from your job ready to open: an email thread you need to reply to, a document of a few pages (a report, a proposal, a policy), and notes from a recent meeting.

---

## About this session

Copilot is very good at the parts of work that are mostly *words*: first drafts, summaries, rewording for a different reader, and turning a messy list into a clean one. It's weak at the part that is mostly *facts*. It doesn't know your company, your clients, or what happened in last Tuesday's meeting unless you tell it. When it doesn't know, it often guesses fluently instead of saying so.

So the whole session follows one habit: **you bring the facts and the judgment; Copilot brings speed.** Every exercise ends with you checking the output against something you know is true.

## 1. Set up, and check you're protected (~10 min)

Open the **Microsoft 365 Copilot app**. It may already be on your work computer; if not, install it from your app store, or use it in a browser at **m365copilot.com**. Sign in with your **work account** and open **Chat**.

Before you type anything, look near the prompt box for a **shield** or a "protected" label. It means you're signed in with your work account, and your chats are covered by your company's data protection. What you type isn't used to train the model, and it stays inside your organization's Microsoft 365 boundary.

**If you don't see the shield, stop.** You're probably signed in with a personal Microsoft account. Sign out and sign in with your work account. A personal account is fine for asking about recipes; it is not the place for a client email.

Even with the shield, three things never go in, whatever account you're on:

- **Passwords, access codes, bank or card numbers.** There's never a reason Copilot needs them.
- **Anything your company's policy says stays out of AI tools.** Ask your manager or IT if you're not sure. That answer beats this lesson.
- **Personal details about people that you wouldn't put in a company-wide email**, such as health, home addresses, or ID numbers. If a task needs them, replace them with placeholders ("[Client A]", "[address]").

Write the three rules on a sticky note. You'll be pasting real work into Copilot in a few minutes.

## 2. What Copilot actually is (~10 min)

Copilot is a **text predictor**. You give it some text, and it predicts what useful text should come next, based on an enormous amount of writing it learned from. That explains almost everything about how it behaves:

- **It's fluent even when it's wrong.** It predicts *plausible* text, and plausible isn't the same as true. A made-up statistic sounds exactly as confident as a real one. This is called **hallucinating**.
- **It only knows what's in the conversation**, plus general knowledge from its training and, sometimes, a web search. It has not read your inbox, your files, or your company's systems unless you paste them in or attach them. (Section 7 covers the paid licence, which can reach your work files.)
- **Ask twice, get two different answers.** Same question, different wording each time. That's normal, so don't treat any single answer as *the* answer.
- **It does what you asked, not what you meant.** Vague request, vague output.

**Predict before you try.** In a note, write one sentence: what do you think Copilot will say if you ask *"What was our company's revenue last year?"* Now ask it. Did it say it doesn't know, guess, or describe a different company? If your prediction was wrong, write `### Edit` under it and say what actually happened. Being wrong and correcting it is the point of the exercise, not a failure.

## 3. Prompts that work (~20 min)

Most disappointing Copilot output comes from a one-line prompt. A good prompt has four parts:

| Part | Question it answers | Example |
|------|---------------------|---------|
| **Goal** | What do you need? | "Draft a reply declining the meeting" |
| **Context** | What does Copilot need to know? | "The client wants to meet Friday; we can't until the 20th; they're a long-standing client" |
| **Format** | What should it look like? | "Under 120 words, three short paragraphs, no bullet points" |
| **Tone / audience** | Who's reading? | "Warm but direct; the reader is a senior manager" |

**Exercise A: weak vs strong.** Send this weak prompt:

> Write an email about the project delay.

Read what comes back. Notice how much it had to invent: which project, how long the delay is, who the email is for, and why it happened.

Now rewrite it with all four parts, using a real situation from your work (with names swapped for placeholders if needed). Send it. Compare the two outputs side by side. In your notes, write down which of the four parts made the biggest difference.

**Exercise B: iterate instead of starting over.** Take the stronger draft and refine it in the same chat with short follow-ups:

> Make it shorter.
> The second paragraph sounds defensive. Make it neutral.
> Add a clear next step at the end with a date.

Each follow-up builds on the last one, because Copilot remembers the conversation. Starting a **new chat** clears that memory. That's useful when you switch tasks, so old context doesn't leak into new work.

**Exercise C: let it ask you.** End a prompt with:

> Before you write anything, ask me up to three questions about what you'd need to know to do this well.

Answer its questions, then let it write. This is the quickest fix for "I don't know what context to give."

## 4. Everyday work: email, documents, meetings (~25 min)

Use the three real things you brought.

**Email (~8 min).** Paste the email thread you need to answer. Ask:

> Summarize what this person is asking for in two sentences. Then list anything they asked that I haven't answered yet.

**Check it:** reread the thread yourself. Did the summary miss a request or invent one? Then ask for a draft reply, giving it your actual answer to each point. **Copilot drafts the wording; you decide the content.** Edit the draft until it sounds like you before it goes anywhere.

**A document (~9 min).** Attach your document (the paperclip / **+** button in the chat), or paste a few pages. Ask:

> Summarize this for a busy executive in five bullet points. After each bullet, quote the sentence from the document it's based on.

The "quote the sentence" part is the important bit. It lets you check every bullet against the source in seconds. **Check it:** find each quote in the document. If a quote isn't really there, or doesn't say what the bullet claims, that bullet is invented. Mark it. Then try a second angle:

> What questions would a skeptical reader have after reading this? What's missing or unclear?

**Meeting notes (~8 min).** Paste your rough notes and ask:

> Turn these notes into: (1) a three-sentence summary, (2) a table of action items with owner and due date, (3) open questions. If an owner or date isn't in my notes, write "not stated". Don't guess.

**Check it:** every owner and date in the table must appear in *your notes*. "Don't guess" helps, but it isn't a guarantee. Copilot may still fill a gap with a plausible name. A wrong owner on an action item causes real problems, so this is the check that matters most.

*Short break here.*

## 5. Catching Copilot when it's wrong (~15 min)

This is the most valuable skill in the session. You'll get Copilot to be wrong on purpose so you recognize it later.

**Exercise A: the confident statistic.** Ask:

> Give me three statistics about [a topic from your industry], with sources.

Then pick one statistic and check it yourself: open the source it named, or search for it. Note what you find. It might be real and accurate, real but different from what Copilot said, or a source that doesn't exist or doesn't contain the number. Any of the last two is a hallucination, delivered in exactly the same confident tone as a correct answer.

**Exercise B: the thin request.** Ask Copilot to write a short case study about a client project in three words: *"case study, successful project."* Read the result. Every specific detail in it (a percentage, a timeline, a quote from a happy client) came from nowhere. It reads like a finished document, and that's what makes it dangerous.

The rule that follows: **facts come from you; Copilot supplies structure and wording.** Before anything Copilot helped write leaves your hands, check every:

- **name** (people, companies, products)
- **number** (prices, dates, percentages, totals)
- **quote** (did that person actually say that?)
- **claim about your company** (policy, history, commitments)

If you can't check it, take it out.

**Exercise C: ask it to check itself, then check anyway.** On one of your earlier drafts, ask:

> Review your draft. List every factual claim in it, and say for each whether it came from what I gave you or from you.

It's a useful first pass, and often catches its own additions. But it can be wrong about itself too. Use it to find what to check, never as the check.

## 6. Numbers and tables (~15 min)

Copilot can read a table you paste and describe patterns in it. Its arithmetic, though, is something to verify, not trust. Use this sample (made-up data, for practice only):

```
Month,Region,Leads,Deals,Revenue
Jan,North,120,18,54000
Jan,South,95,11,30800
Feb,North,130,21,63000
Feb,South,88,9,26100
Mar,North,110,15,46500
Mar,South,102,16,49600
```

**Predict first:** before asking anything, look at the table and write down which region you think did better in March, and roughly why. Then paste it and ask:

> Here's a table. Which region had the better conversion rate (deals divided by leads) each month? What's the trend? Show your working for March.

**Check one number by hand.** March South: 16 ÷ 102 = about 15.7%. March North: 15 ÷ 110 = about 13.6%. Does Copilot's March match? Did your prediction? If either was off, write `### Edit` in your notes and say why. A wrong guess from just looking at the numbers is useful to know about.

Then try:

> Write two sentences for a manager summarizing this, without overstating anything. Three months is not much data.

The second sentence of that prompt is your judgment, and Copilot won't add it on its own. With three months of numbers, "South is catching up" is a fair reading; "South will overtake North" is not.

For real spreadsheets, the same rule applies: Copilot is fast at "what stands out here?" and "write a formula that does X". Check any total, average or percentage it gives you against the spreadsheet itself before you repeat it to anyone.

## 7. What else exists — the bigger picture (~10 min)

Read this section; there's nothing to install.

**If your company has a paid Microsoft 365 Copilot licence**, Copilot also appears *inside* Outlook, Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Teams. It can work with your actual work files and meetings (only ones you already have permission to see), and do things like summarize a Teams meeting you missed or draft a document from other files. Look for the Copilot button in those apps; if it isn't there, you're on the free chat, and everything in this session still applies. Same rules either way: a summary of your own meeting can still invent an action item.

**For developers there's GitHub Copilot**, a different product with the same family name. The real developer tool is **Copilot CLI**: Copilot running in the terminal inside a code project. It reads the project's files, proposes changes as before-and-after comparisons ("diffs"), and asks permission before running anything. The full 12-week course in this repo teaches it from zero. The habits you practised today come straight from there: check before you accept, the human decides, never wave through what you can't explain.

**Questions to take back to your manager or IT:**

- Do we have the paid Microsoft 365 Copilot licence, or the free Copilot Chat?
- Is there a written policy on what can and can't go into AI tools?
- Are there approved prompts or templates for our common tasks?

## 8. Your rules for work (~5 min)

Turn the session into a short personal checklist. Write five rules you'll actually follow, in your own words. Start from these and adapt them:

1. Work account, shield visible — or I don't paste work content.
2. Goal, context, format, audience — every prompt that matters gets all four.
3. I write (or at least know) the answer's key facts *before* I ask.
4. Every name, number, date and quote is checked before it leaves my hands.
5. Copilot drafts; I decide and I sign.

## Journal (~10 min)

Write this in a note or document you'll keep:

```
# Copilot for Business

## What I did
(short list: the three real tasks I worked on)

## The prompt that changed the most
Paste the weak and strong versions of one prompt. Which of the four parts
(goal, context, format, audience) made the biggest difference, and why?

## Where Copilot was wrong
One specific thing Copilot got wrong today — a missed request, an invented
quote, a bad statistic, a guessed owner. Quote it, and say how you caught it.

## My prediction vs what happened
What did you predict in section 2 or section 6? What actually happened?
(### Edit if you were wrong — say what you got wrong and why.)

## What I don't fully understand
Name one specific thing about how Copilot behaves — a response you couldn't
explain, a feature you weren't sure about, where your data goes — that you'd
want to ask about. Specific beats general: "why did it give two different
owners for the same task?" is useful; "AI is confusing" is not.

## Where I'll use it this week
Two real tasks from your job next week where you'll use Copilot, and the
check you'll run on each.
```

## What "done" looks like

- The Microsoft 365 Copilot app is signed in with your work account, and you know what the shield means
- You can name the three things that never go into Copilot
- You rewrote a weak prompt using goal, context, format and audience, and saw the difference
- You drafted a real email reply, summarized a real document with quotes, and turned real meeting notes into action items — and checked each against the source
- You caught at least one Copilot mistake (a statistic, a quote, an invented detail, or a guessed owner)
- You checked one of Copilot's numbers by hand
- You have five written rules for using Copilot at work, and a journal entry naming one specific thing you don't fully understand yet
